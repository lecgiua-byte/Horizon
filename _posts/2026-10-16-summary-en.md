### [A Shortage of Sulphur Could Hurt Mines and Farms Everywhere](https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2026/10/15/a-shortage-of-sulphur-could-hurt-mines-and-farms-everywhere) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The Economist reports that a potential sulphur shortage, linked to the Iran war, could disrupt global mining and agriculture. Sulphur is a critical input for fertilizer production and mining processes, so any supply disruption could raise costs across food and metals supply chains. The source provides no specific figures, dates, or named companies, so the scale and timing of the shortage remain uncertain.

rss · The Economist · Oct 15, 09:32

**「Background」** Sulphur is a basic industrial input, and most of the world&\#x27;s supply is not mined for its own sake. The overwhelming majority is recovered as a by-product of processing fossil fuels — mainly refining crude oil and, especially, &quot;sour&quot; natural gas that is rich in hydrogen sulphide. That means sulphur supply is largely determined by oil and gas activity rather than by sulphur demand itself, so it can tighten sharply when energy flows are disrupted.

Its main uses explain why a shortage would travel far beyond chemicals. Roughly half of global sulphur consumption goes into sulphuric acid, which is the key intermediate for phosphate fertilizers. Sulphuric acid is also central to leaching and processing many metals, including copper and nickel, and to a range of industrial processes. So sulphur sits underneath both the food system and the mining system.

The specific trigger here is the Iran war. According to BC Insight \(tool-1-1\), US and Israeli attacks on Iran and the Iranian response have thrown commodity markets into chaos, with sulphur and sulphuric acid particularly affected. Iran and the wider Gulf are significant producers and exporters of sulphur recovered from oil and gas, and the region also sits on major shipping routes. Conflict can therefore hit supply in two ways: by disrupting production and by raising the cost and risk of moving material out.

This is not an isolated event. It follows a period in which critical-mineral and commodity supply security has become a central policy concern; the IEA&\#x27;s 2026 Global Critical Minerals Outlook \(tool-1-2\) documents the broader push to assess market, investment and technology trends and their implications for supply security. Sulphur is not usually classified as a critical mineral, but it behaves like one for fertilizer and metal producers because substitutes are limited in the short run.

Price data is available but should be treated cautiously. Price-watch.ai \(tool-1-3\) offers global and regional sulphur price trends, historical pricing by country and a 12-month forecast, which is the kind of source that can help track whether a disruption is translating into sustained higher prices rather than a brief spike. The source item itself is only a headline — &quot;Another commodity shock from the Iran war&quot; — so the scale, duration and actual price impact remain unverified.

**「Commercial Signal: Sulphur Supply Disruption」** \*\*What the evidence supports\*\*

The source item is extremely thin: a headline and a one-line summary stating that a sulphur shortage linked to the Iran war could hurt mines and farms. There is no price data, no volume figure, no named company, no date beyond the article&\#x27;s publication. That limits how far any opportunity analysis can go.

Supporting search results add some texture. One result \(tool-2-1\) reports that rises in elemental sulphur prices have caused disruptions across the supply chain, affecting fertilizer and industrial manufacturing. Another \(tool-2-2\) estimates that recycling and alternative sourcing strategies could alleviate 25–30% of projected supply constraints. A third \(tool-2-3\) links the U.S.–Israel war with Iran to disrupted supply chains and rising prices for fertilizer raw materials including sulphur and ammonia, and references a phosphate producer pulling guidance and cutting spending.

These are secondary reports, not primary data. They are consistent with the source&\#x27;s claim but do not independently verify its scale or duration.

\*\*Where a real pain point may exist\*\*

Sulphur is a feedstock for sulphuric acid, which is used in phosphate fertilizer production and in mining processes such as copper leaching and uranium extraction. If supply is genuinely constrained, the buyers who feel it first are:

\- Phosphate fertilizer producers, who need sulphuric acid as a direct input.
\- Mining companies, particularly copper and uranium operations that rely on acid leaching.
\- Industrial manufacturers using sulphuric acid in chemicals, metals processing, or water treatment.

These are large, concentrated buyers with existing procurement budgets. They are the most likely to pay for supply security, recycling, or substitution.

\*\*Possible commercial angles — with caveats\*\*

\1. \*\*Sulphur recovery and recycling.\*\* Tool-2-2 suggests recycling could offset a meaningful share of a shortage. Companies that already recover sulphur from oil and gas processing, or that can economically extract it from waste streams, could see higher demand for their output. A small team would struggle to build recovery infrastructure, but consulting or process-engineering services for existing operators could be a lower-capital entry point.

\2. \*\*Alternative sourcing and logistics.\*\* If traditional supply routes are disrupted, traders and logistics providers who can move sulphur or sulphuric acid from alternative producers may capture margin. This is a trading and relationship business, not a software business. It requires capital, permits, and handling expertise — not obviously suitable for a small individual.

\3. \*\*Substitution and efficiency in fertilizer production.\*\* Fertilizer producers facing higher sulphur costs may look for process improvements that reduce acid consumption per tonne of phosphate. This is a specialized engineering problem, likely served by existing equipment and chemical suppliers rather than new entrants.

\4. \*\*Data and price transparency.\*\* In a disrupted commodity market, reliable price and availability data becomes more valuable. A small team could potentially build a niche price-reporting or supply-tracking service for sulphur and sulphuric acid. But this is speculative: the source provides no evidence that buyers are currently paying for such data, and established commodity price reporting agencies already cover many industrial chemicals.

\*\*Who might benefit or be threatened\*\*

Potential beneficiaries, if the shortage is real and sustained: sulphur producers outside the disrupted region, recycling technology providers, and traders with alternative supply chains. Potential losers: phosphate fertilizer producers with no hedging, mining operations dependent on acid leaching, and farmers facing higher fertilizer costs.

\*\*Cheapest way to test\*\*

Before building anything, verify the shortage is real and persistent. Check published sulphur and sulphuric acid price indices over the past 6–12 months. Talk to procurement managers at phosphate producers or copper miners about whether they are actually paying more or struggling to secure supply. If prices are elevated but buyers are not changing behaviour, the opportunity is likely a trading story, not a new-business story.

\*\*Evidence that would confirm a real opportunity\*\*

\- Sustained price increases in sulphur and sulphuric acid, not a one-week spike.
\- Named producers publicly reporting supply constraints or guidance cuts \(tool-2-3 hints at this but is not sufficient on its own\).
\- New contracts or investments in sulphur recovery or alternative sourcing.
\- Buyers explicitly stating they are willing to pay for recycling, substitution, or supply-chain services.

\*\*Bottom line\*\*

The source alone does not support a specific entrepreneurial opportunity. The supporting results suggest a plausible supply-chain disruption with real cost pressure on fertilizer and mining, but the evidence is secondary and incomplete. The most credible commercial activity is in existing industrial supply chains — recovery, trading, and process efficiency — not in a new consumer-facing product. A small team or individual could only participate credibly through specialized consulting, data, or brokerage services, and only after independently verifying that the shortage is real and that buyers are actually changing their spending.

**「Risks and Caveats」** \*\*Weak evidence in the source itself.\*\* The Economist item is a single line — &quot;Another commodity shock from the Iran war&quot; — with no figures on sulphur volumes, price moves, affected trade routes, or duration. Any commercial conclusion drawn from it is inference, not established fact. Treat the shortage as a hypothesis until harder data appears.

\*\*Hype and narrative risk.\*\* Commodity &quot;shortage&quot; stories attract speculative positioning. Sulphur is a byproduct of oil and gas refining, so supply can rebound quickly if refining runs recover or trade routes reopen. A price spike may be temporary rather than structural, which would strand anyone who invests in new capacity or inventory at the top.

\*\*Regulatory and geopolitical risk.\*\* Sulphur and sulphuric acid are hazardous materials with strict transport, storage and environmental rules. Any sourcing, recycling or trading venture faces permitting, safety and liability exposure. The underlying trigger is a war, so the disruption could end abruptly through diplomacy or escalate unpredictably — both outcomes are hard to hedge.

\*\*Competition and incumbency.\*\* Large fertilizer and mining companies already have procurement teams, long-term contracts and alternative suppliers. A small entrant competing on spot sourcing is likely to be outbid or squeezed on margin. Established producers can also substitute or vertically integrate faster than a new player can scale.

\*\*Capital and logistics intensity.\*\* Sulphur recovery, acid production and bulk shipping are capital-heavy, with long lead times and thin margins. This is not a business easily tested by an individual without significant capital or an existing logistics network.

\*\*Timing risk.\*\* If the shortage is short-lived, demand for recycling, alternative supply or consulting evaporates before a new venture reaches break-even. Conversely, if it persists, governments may intervene with export controls or subsidies that reshape the market against new entrants.

\*\*Evidence still missing.\*\* There is no supplied data on price levels, inventory, trade flows or contract terms. Without that, claims about who will pay and how much are speculative. The apparent opportunity may fail simply because the disruption is smaller, shorter or better-managed than the headline implies.
