### [OpenAI Claims AI Progress in Mathematics: 90 of Top 500 Open Problems Solved](https://openai.com/index/sharing-ai-progress-in-mathematics/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

OpenAI published a GitHub repository claiming AI-generated solutions to 90 of the top 500 open problems in mathematics, including high-profile problems such as Hilbert&\#x27;s tenth problem over Q, the Unique Games Conjecture, and Barnette&\#x27;s Conjecture. If verified, this would mark a significant acceleration in AI-driven mathematical discovery and could reshape research workflows and commercial applications in automated theorem proving and scientific AI. However, the claims remain unverified by the broader mathematical community, and the timeline for practical impact is uncertain.

**「Background」** OpenAI published a set of results on open problems in mathematics, generated by an internal frontier model, and released Lean proof formalizations and research details on GitHub \(tool-1-2\). The source item links to two GitHub locations: a repository at github.com/openai/math and a preprints folder within it. The item&\#x27;s analysis summary states that OpenAI claims solutions to 90 of the top 500 open math problems, including Hilbert&\#x27;s tenth problem over Q and the Unique Games Conjecture, but notes these claims are unverified.

A community commenter \(zone411\) says a quick check of the list shows 90 of the top 500 open problems are claimed as fully solved, with the highest-ranked including Hilbert&\#x27;s tenth problem over Q \(rank 22\), Unique Games \(29\), Anderson-model extended states \(31\), the spacetime Penrose inequality \(37\), nonexistence of Landau–Siegel zeros \(48\), Baum–Connes \(52\), Abundance \(78\), Hadwiger \(80\), Bose–Einstein condensation \(87\), and a two-dimensional entanglement item \(92\). Another commenter \(prideout\) says the set includes a proof of Barnette&\#x27;s Conjecture, a graph theory problem they had tried with state-of-the-art models and failed. A third \(NotOscarWilde\) notes a polynomial-time algorithm for three-machine unit-job scheduling, open since the 1979 Garey and Johnson book. A fourth \(enoether\) describes the Unique Games Conjecture as a seminal complexity-theory conjecture underlying many inapproximability results.

Context on why this matters: mathematics has become a testbed for AI reasoning because proofs are checkable. Formal proof assistants such as Lean allow machine-verifiable proofs, which is why OpenAI released Lean formalizations rather than only prose. The community reaction mixes interest with caution: commenter xanderlewis quotes Kevin Buzzard reflecting on how much further one mind with total knowledge of modern pure mathematics could see, suggesting the field is beginning to understand the answer. The tool results add that OpenAI&\#x27;s own page describes publishing results from an internal frontier model with Lean formalizations and research details on GitHub \(tool-1-2\). Other tool results are not relevant background: one is a TikTok discovery page about OpenAI solving a math problem with 10,000 AI agents, 88 hours, and millions of dollars in compute for a $1M prize \(tool-1-1\), and another is a consumer AI math-solver product claiming over 2M students in 150+ countries \(tool-1-3\). Neither is used here as verified context for OpenAI&\#x27;s mathematical claims.

Key uncertainty: the source item and comments are claims and reactions, not independent verification. The analysis summary itself flags the claims as unverified and the timeline for broad impact as uncertain.

**「Commercial Signal: Verification, Not Proof Generation」** \*\*What the evidence actually supports\*\*

The source item is an OpenAI announcement that it has shared AI-generated work on mathematics, including a GitHub repository with preprints. Community commenters claim this includes full solutions to 90 of the top 500 open problems, with the highest-ranked being Hilbert&\#x27;s tenth problem over Q, Unique Games, and others. These are source-community claims, not independently verified facts. The analysis summary itself notes the claims are unverified.

That distinction matters commercially. If the proofs are correct, the bottleneck shifts from \*finding\* proofs to \*checking, formalizing, and integrating\* them. If they are wrong or incomplete, the bottleneck is \*detecting\* that. Either way, the near-term demand is for verification and formalization, not for another proof generator.

\*\*Where the pain point is\*\*

Mathematical proofs written in natural language are not machine-checkable. The standard tooling for formal verification \(Lean, Coq, Isabelle\) requires proofs to be translated into formal languages, which is slow, specialized work. A flood of AI-generated natural-language proofs — even a few dozen — creates a backlog of claims that no human can quickly validate. Journals, research groups, and anyone relying on these results need a way to triage them.

Who has this problem:
\- Mathematics journals and preprint servers facing a wave of AI-assisted submissions.
\- University research groups trying to build on claimed results.
\- Companies using formal methods \(hardware verification, cryptography, safety-critical software\) that depend on trusted proofs.
\- AI labs themselves, which need external validation to make their claims credible.

\*\*Who might pay\*\*

Paying customers are less likely to be individual mathematicians and more likely to be institutions: publishers, universities, national labs, defense and aerospace contractors, chip designers, and AI companies. These are buyers with existing budgets for formal verification and research integrity.

\*\*Possible products and services\*\*

\1. \*\*Proof triage and formalization services.\*\* Take a natural-language proof, attempt to formalize it in Lean or a similar system, and report where it fails. This is consulting or software-as-a-service work. A small team with Lean expertise could start here.
\2. \*\*Verification tooling.\*\* Semi-automated translation of natural-language mathematical arguments into formal statements, with human review. This is harder and more capital-intensive, but the demand signal is real if AI proof output keeps growing.
\3. \*\*Reputation and provenance layer.\*\* A service that tracks which claimed results have been formally verified, by whom, and to what standard. This is a data and trust product. It only works if there is enough volume of contested claims.
\4. \*\*Benchmark and evaluation services.\*\* Independent testing of AI math claims for labs, publishers, or regulators. This is closer to an auditing business.

\*\*Could a small team participate?\*\*

Yes, at the service layer. Formalization and verification are skill-intensive but not capital-intensive. The cheapest test is to take a handful of the claimed proofs from the public repository, attempt to formalize them in Lean, and document where the process breaks down. That produces a concrete artifact — a verification report — that can be shown to potential customers. If no one wants to pay for that report, the opportunity is weak.

\*\*Could a proven model transfer across countries?\*\*

Formal verification is already an international research activity. A verification service built around Lean or Coq could serve customers in any country with a research or formal-methods community. The constraint is talent, not geography.

\*\*Evidence that would confirm the opportunity\*\*

\- Independent mathematicians confirming or refuting specific claimed proofs.
\- Journals or preprint servers announcing policies for AI-generated proofs.
\- AI labs hiring or contracting for external verification.
\- Growth in job postings for Lean or formal verification skills.
\- Paid pilots for proof-triage services.

\*\*What would falsify it\*\*

\- The claimed proofs turn out to be wrong, trivial, or restatements of known results.
\- Formalization turns out to be cheap and automated, removing the service need.
\- The volume of AI-generated proofs stays low enough that existing peer review absorbs it.

\*\*Bottom line\*\*

The credible near-term opportunity is in verification, formalization, and trust infrastructure for AI-generated mathematics — not in generating more proofs. This is a service and tooling opportunity suited to small, specialized teams. It is not a mass-market software opportunity, and it depends on the unverified claims in the source item being at least partly real.

**「Risks and Caveats」** \*\*Hype and weak evidence.\*\* The central claim — that OpenAI&\#x27;s system solved 90 of the top 500 open problems, including Hilbert&\#x27;s tenth over Q and Unique Games — comes from OpenAI&\#x27;s own release and a community member&\#x27;s quick check of a list. The proofs are posted as preprints, not peer-reviewed publications. No independent verification is cited in the supplied material. Treat the headline numbers as unconfirmed.

\*\*Verification crisis.\*\* Community discussion \(tool-3-1\) explicitly raises the prospect of a &quot;verification crisis&quot; from a large volume of AI-generated papers. If hundreds of proofs arrive faster than mathematicians can check them, the bottleneck shifts from discovery to validation. That cuts both ways: it may create demand for verification tools, but it also means many claimed results could sit unverified for a long time, and some may turn out to be wrong or trivial.

\*\*Regulatory and academic risk.\*\* Mathematics has no formal regulator, but academic credit, journal acceptance and tenure decisions depend on peer review. If AI-generated proofs are not accepted by journals or are seen as low-status, commercial demand for them may stay small. Conversely, if institutions ban or restrict AI-generated submissions, adoption could stall.

\*\*Competition.\*\* OpenAI is not alone. Other labs, university groups and open-source projects are working on automated theorem proving and AI for mathematics. Any tool or service built on top of this progress faces fast-moving competition and possible commoditization.

\*\*Capital and talent requirements.\*\* Building frontier reasoning models or formal verification systems requires large compute budgets and scarce specialist talent. A small team or individual is unlikely to compete at the frontier. Opportunities are more plausible in adjacent niches — verification, tooling, data curation, consulting — than in core model development.

\*\*Timing risk.\*\* The timeline for broad commercial impact is uncertain. Even if the proofs are correct, translating them into products, standards or workflows could take years. Early movers may burn cash before demand materializes.

\*\*Reasons the apparent opportunity may fail.\*\* The proofs may not survive scrutiny; the market for mathematical results may be too small to support standalone businesses; formal verification may remain a niche academic practice; and the strongest players may bundle verification and proving into existing platforms, leaving little room for independents.
