### [Federal Reserve Seeks Comment on Stablecoin Rules Under the GENIUS Act](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/bcreg20260924a.htm) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The Federal Reserve Board has requested public comment on two proposals to establish a regulatory framework for payment stablecoin issuers that it supervises under the GENIUS Act. This matters because clear federal rules could reduce legal uncertainty for stablecoin issuance, shaping how compliant issuers, payments firms, and fintech infrastructure companies operate in the United States.

rss · Federal Reserve - Press Releases · Sep 24, 18:30

**「Background」** The GENIUS Act, signed into law in 2025, is the first US federal law creating a comprehensive regulatory framework for payment stablecoins. It amends federal securities laws and the Commodity Exchange Act to classify payment stablecoins as neither &quot;securities&quot; nor &quot;commodities,&quot; leaving their regulation to banking regulators rather than the SEC or CFTC \(tool-1-1\). This matters because it removes a major source of legal ambiguity that had previously deterred banks and mainstream financial firms from issuing or holding stablecoins.

The Act sets up a category of &quot;permitted payment stablecoin issuers&quot; and assigns oversight to federal banking agencies, including the Federal Reserve for entities it supervises \(tool-1-2\). However, the statute itself is a framework, not a complete rulebook. It leaves key operational questions to be resolved through agency rulemaking — for example, whether permitted issuers will have access to Federal Reserve accounts, which is central to how stablecoin reserves are held and redeemed \(tool-1-2\).

The Federal Reserve&\#x27;s request for public comment on two proposals is therefore a procedural step in translating the GENIUS Act&\#x27;s broad mandates into concrete, enforceable rules for the issuers it supervises. Public comment periods are a standard part of US agency rulemaking: they allow industry, consumer groups, and the public to shape the final rules before they take effect. The proposals themselves, their specific contents, and the timeline for finalization are not detailed in the source item, so the practical impact on issuers remains uncertain until the rules are finalized.

**「Commercial Signal: Stablecoin Regulation Moves from Theory to Rulebook」** \*\*What the signal actually is\*\*

The Federal Reserve has opened two proposals for public comment on how it would supervise payment stablecoin issuers under the GENIUS Act. According to supporting coverage, the first proposal would require full reserve backing and set standardized capital, risk management, and custody requirements; the second would create a tailored application process for Fed-regulated banks to issue stablecoins \(tool-2-2, tool-2-3\). The comment period is reported as 60 days \(tool-2-2\). Separately, the OCC has already issued its own GENIUS Act implementation proposal for entities under its jurisdiction, with comments due May 1, 2026 \(tool-2-1\).

This is a rulemaking step, not a market launch. No product is approved, no issuer is licensed by this action, and the final rules could change materially after comment.

\*\*Where the real demand is\*\*

The clearest pain point is compliance uncertainty. Banks and non-bank issuers that want to issue payment stablecoins currently face unclear federal treatment. The proposals, if finalized, would replace some of that ambiguity with concrete reserve, capital, custody, and application requirements.

Who has this problem:
\- Fed-supervised banks considering stablecoin issuance.
\- Existing stablecoin issuers seeking a federal path.
\- Custodians, auditors, and compliance vendors serving those issuers.
\- Payment companies and fintechs that want to use regulated stablecoins.

Who is likely willing to pay: institutions that cannot enter the market without a clear legal framework. That is a real, budgeted need, but it is concentrated among a relatively small number of large regulated entities, not a mass market.

\*\*Plausible commercial angles\*\*

\1. Compliance and reporting tooling. Full reserve backing, standardized capital, and custody requirements imply ongoing attestation, reserve reporting, and audit workflows. Software that automates reserve reconciliation, disclosure, and regulatory reporting could be sold to issuers and their custodians. This is a B2B software opportunity, not a consumer one.

\2. Application and licensing support. The tailored application process for Fed-regulated banks creates demand for advisory and documentation work. This is likely a consulting or legal-tech niche, best suited to firms with existing bank regulatory expertise.

\3. Custody and infrastructure. Standardized custody requirements may push issuers toward qualified custodians and specialized infrastructure providers. Incumbents with bank trust charters or existing custody relationships are better positioned than startups.

\4. Cross-border model transfer. The U.S. framework resembles emerging stablecoin regimes elsewhere \(for example, reserve-backed regimes in major jurisdictions\). A compliance or reporting product built for one regime could potentially be adapted to another, but this is inference, not confirmed demand.

\*\*Can a small team participate?\*\*

Possibly, but narrowly. The most accessible entry point is not issuing stablecoins \(capital-intensive, license-dependent\) but building compliance, reporting, or monitoring software for issuers and custodians. A small team could prototype a reserve-attestation or regulatory-reporting tool. However, the customer base is small and slow-moving, sales cycles are long, and the rules are not final. A small team would be selling into uncertainty.

\*\*Cheapest way to test\*\*

\- Read the actual proposals and the OCC proposal \(tool-2-1\) and map the specific reporting and reserve requirements.
\- Interview a handful of compliance officers at banks or custodians about what they currently do manually.
\- Build a narrow prototype around one concrete requirement \(for example, reserve reconciliation or disclosure formatting\) and test willingness to pay.

\*\*Evidence that would confirm the opportunity\*\*

\- Final rules that impose specific, recurring reporting or attestation obligations.
\- Public statements from banks or custodians about building or buying stablecoin compliance tooling.
\- Job postings for stablecoin compliance, reserve, or reporting roles at regulated institutions.
\- Actual applications filed under the new framework.

\*\*What is not supported\*\*

There is no evidence here of consumer demand, token price effects, or a broad retail market. The source is a policy consultation. Treating it as an immediate business opportunity would be premature.

\*\*Bottom line\*\*

A credible but early-stage B2B opportunity exists in compliance, reporting, and custody infrastructure for regulated stablecoin issuers. It is not yet actionable at scale because the rules are proposals, not final law, and the paying customer base is small and institutional. The strongest near-term play is information and expertise, not capital deployment.

**「Risks and Caveats」** \*\*This is a proposal, not a final rule.\*\* The Federal Reserve is only requesting public comment. The framework could change substantially before adoption, and the timeline is uncertain. Under the GENIUS Act, final rules trigger the law&\#x27;s effective date \(the earlier of 18 months after enactment or 120 days after primary regulators issue final regulations\), so the practical start date depends on how fast regulators move \(tool-3-2\).

\*\*Regulatory risk cuts both ways.\*\* Stricter-than-expected rules on reserves, AML, and licensing could raise compliance costs and squeeze smaller players, even as they legitimize the market \(tool-3-1, tool-3-3\). Foreign issuers face separate participation standards, adding cross-border complexity \(tool-3-1\).

\*\*Hype risk.\*\* The source item is a procedural press release with no details on the proposals&\#x27; content. Treat any specific business opportunity as speculative until the actual text and comment outcomes are known.

\*\*Competition and capital.\*\* Stablecoin issuance is capital- and trust-intensive, with incumbent issuers and large banks likely to dominate compliant issuance. A small team is unlikely to become a licensed issuer; realistic roles are in tooling, compliance, and services.

\*\*Timing risk.\*\* Acting too early on an unfinalized framework risks building for rules that never materialize or change. Acting too late risks losing first-mover advantage.

\*\*Weak evidence for near-term payoff.\*\* No market data, adoption figures, or final rule text is provided here. The commercial signal is directional, not yet actionable.
